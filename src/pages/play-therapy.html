Play therapy

Play is a child's language

Play therapy uses play to help children understand themselves, and along the way gives them strategies to cope with the difficulties they face in life.

What it involves

Weekly "play time", just for them

Your child gets "play time" every week at the same time and place. Sessions have no interruptions, and everything said or done stays confidential, unless the child is being harmed or hurt.

The journey

The stages of play therapy

Through play, children learn to identify and express their emotions, improve social and communication skills, develop self-regulation and solve problems. A typical process looks like this:

  1. 1

    Weeks 1 to 8

    Building trust

    Your child becomes comfortable sharing their inner world. They test boundaries, and test whether the therapist can keep them safe.

  2. 2

    Weeks 8 to 12

    Expressing needs

    The real concerns become the focus. This can be the hardest stage: children may have more outbursts, want more control, or slip back in things they had mastered. More than ever, they need your support, nurture and love.

  3. 3

    Weeks 12 to 18

    Solving problems

    Your child starts using coping skills and self-regulation, and you may see changes at school and at home. Small setbacks are normal: it takes about 3 months to form a new neural pathway.

  4. 4

    Weeks 18 to 22

    A good goodbye

    The therapist prepares your child for the end of therapy, because endings aren't all bad. Please avoid ending therapy early or unplanned.

  5. {{IC:sprout}}

    After therapy

    Expect a few minor regressions, but generally children adapt and recognise the strength they have inside themselves.

A typical process lasts 12 to 22 weeks, but can be longer. Every child's needs are unique.

Need to know for parents

A safe space to play, or not

  • {{IC:lock}}

    Confidentiality

    A confidentiality contract is set up with your child in the first session, and feedback respects their inner world. If abuse or neglect is suspected, the therapist must by law report it to a social worker or the SAPS.

  • {{IC:shield}}

    Rules in the playroom

    Only a few boundaries keep the child, therapist and toys safe. Therapy is non-directive: the therapist rarely raises things that happened outside the room.

  • {{IC:splash}}

    Play can be messy

    Children are free to make a mess. Please dress them in clothes and shoes that may get paint on them.

Questions

Questions parents ask

How long is a session, and how often?

A session is 40 minutes, once a week: the same day, same time and same place.

We're divorced and don't speak. Can I give permission on my own?

No, both parents need to give permission. It's always in your child's best interest to have both parents involved, but even if one parent doesn't want to take part, both still need to agree to therapy.

When will I get feedback?

The first feedback session is usually after six to eight sessions.

Do you write reports for the school, or for legal use?

School reports, yes; legal use, no. Reports are available on request (additional fees apply), and you may share them with your child's teacher. Therapy is not a forensic assessment, so the information isn't used for legal purposes.

How can I support my child during therapy?

Be sensitive to their needs after a session, don't ask what they did in the session, and be understanding about the difficulties they're facing.

How do I pay?

It's a cash practice: invoices are sent monthly, and the whole month's sessions are paid by the end of each month. You can also pay weekly or upfront if you prefer.
